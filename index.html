Nature-Inspired Data Storage Systems

Nature has been storing and improving information for billions of years. DNA keeps the instructions of every living thing, and evolution keeps finding better answers by trial and selection. This page has two small labs where you can try both ideas yourself.

1. DNA-Based Storage Systems

Type a message, turn it into A, C, G, T letters, damage it with copying errors, and see if it can be recovered.

2. Genetic Algorithm and its application

Watch a population of "cargo plans" evolve over generations to pack the most valuable load under a weight limit.

Why these two together?

Both are copied from nature. DNA is how nature stores data. A genetic algorithm is how nature searches for the best choice, like what is best to keep in limited space.

Part 1DNA-Based Storage Systems

Normal computers store data as 0s and 1s. DNA uses four letters instead: A, C, G, T. So we can write any file in those four letters, make the DNA in a lab, and read it back later by sequencing.

How it works

  1. Convert the message into bits (0 and 1).
  2. Cut the bits into pairs. Each pair becomes one base: 00=A 01=C 10=G 11=T.
  3. Synthesize DNA with that order of letters.
  4. Later, sequence the DNA and convert the letters back to bits and text.

Why people care

  • Very dense: researchers estimate about 215 petabytes can fit in one gram of DNA (Erlich & Zielinski, 2017).
  • Lasts long: DNA kept cool and dry can survive for thousands of years, much longer than hard disks.
  • Problems: writing and reading DNA is slow and costly today, and errors happen. That is why we keep extra copies and vote, which you can try below.

Part 2Genetic Algorithm and its application

A genetic algorithm (GA) copies Darwin's idea. Start with many random answers, keep the better ones, mix them, change them a little, and repeat. After many generations the answers get very good.

The steps in nature words

  1. Population: a group of random answers (each answer is a "chromosome" of 0s and 1s).
  2. Fitness: give each answer a score. Higher is better.
  3. Selection: better answers get picked as parents more often.
  4. Crossover: a child takes part of its genes from each parent.
  5. Mutation: a few genes flip at random to try new ideas.
  6. Repeat for many generations.

Where it is used

  • Packing and storage: choosing which items or files fit best in limited space (the knapsack problem, which you can try below).
  • Timetables and scheduling: exam and shift plans with many rules.
  • Routes: delivery and travelling-salesman style problems.
  • Engineering design: NASA used an evolved antenna shape on its ST5 spacecraft.

Link to storage: deciding what to keep on a drive with limited space is the same type of problem as loading a truck.

SummaryWhat we learned

DNA storage

Data can be written in 4 letters, stored very densely, and recovered even after errors if we keep extra copies and check them against each other.

Genetic algorithm

A simple copy of evolution can solve a hard packing problem without trying every combination.

Main idea

Nature is a good teacher for storing data and for choosing the best option. These ideas are already used in research and industry.
